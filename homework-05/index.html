Theoretical Background

Population and Sample Statistics

A population is described by a probability distribution with theoretical mean mu and variance sigma squared.

A sample consists of n observations drawn from that population. Its sample mean and sample variance are used to estimate the corresponding population parameters.

Population mean: mu = E[X].

Population variance: sigma² = E[(X - mu)²].

Sample Mean

For observations x(1), ..., x(n), the sample mean is:

x_bar = (1/n) * sum(x(i)).

Under independent and identically distributed sampling, the sample mean is an unbiased estimator of mu.

Its theoretical variance is:

Var(x_bar) = sigma² / n.

Sample Variance

The sample variance measures the dispersion of observations around their sample mean.

The unbiased sample variance is:

s² = sum((x(i) - x_bar)²) / (n - 1).

The denominator n - 1 is known as Bessel's correction. For IID observations with finite variance, this estimator satisfies E[s²] = sigma².

Sample variance is defined for at least two observations.

Online Algorithms

An online algorithm processes observations sequentially, updating its statistics as new data arrives.

Knuth's recurrence updates the running mean without storing the complete sample. Welford's method extends this idea to the sum of squared deviations and sample variance.

These methods are particularly useful for streaming data and large datasets, where storing all observations may be unnecessary or expensive.

Inferential Statistics

Inferential statistics uses sample data to estimate unknown population properties and quantify uncertainty.

By repeating the sampling experiment, we can study the sampling distributions of the mean and variance estimators.

Their averages and variability help evaluate estimator bias, precision and consistency.

Exercise 1 — Welford's Recurrence Formulas

Mathematical Derivation

Welford's algorithm computes the running mean, sum of squared deviations and sample variance using recurrence formulas.

At each iteration, the algorithm processes one new observation without storing the previous observations.

Step 1 — Knuth's Recurrence for the Mean

For observations x(1), ..., x(n), the sample mean is:

mean(n) = (1/n) * sum(x(i)).

Separating the last observation:

mean(n) = [(n-1) * mean(n-1) + x(n)] / n.

Rearranging:

mean(n) = mean(n-1) + [x(n) - mean(n-1)] / n.

Define delta = x(n) - mean(n-1). Then:

mean(n) = mean(n-1) + delta/n.

Step 2 — Welford's Sum of Squares Recurrence

Define the sum of squared deviations:

M2(n) = sum[(x(i) - mean(n))²].

Separate the contribution of the newest observation:

M2(n) = sum[i=1 to n-1](x(i) - mean(n))² + (x(n) - mean(n))².

From Knuth's recurrence:

mean(n) - mean(n-1) = delta/n.

Therefore, for every previous observation:

x(i) - mean(n) = [x(i) - mean(n-1)] - delta/n.

Expanding the square and summing, the cross term vanishes because:

sum[i=1 to n-1] (x(i) - mean(n-1)) = 0.

The previous observations contribute:

M2(n-1) + (n-1)*delta²/n².

The newest observation contributes:

(x(n) - mean(n))² = [(n-1)*delta/n]².

Combining the terms:

M2(n) = M2(n-1) + [(n-1)/n]*delta².

Since x(n) - mean(n) = [(n-1)/n]*delta, we obtain Welford's recurrence:

M2(n) = M2(n-1) + delta * [x(n) - mean(n)].

Step 3 — Sample Variance Recurrence

The unbiased sample variance is:

s²(n) = M2(n) / (n-1), for n >= 2.

Substituting the recurrence for M2:

s²(n) = [M2(n-1) + delta*(x(n)-mean(n))] / (n-1).

Because M2(n-1) = (n-2)*s²(n-1), for n >= 3 we obtain:

s²(n) = [(n-2)*s²(n-1) + delta*(x(n)-mean(n))] / (n-1).

For n = 2, the sample variance is M2(2), since the denominator is 1. For n = 1, sample variance is undefined.

Step 4 — Three-Line Core Implementation

After incrementing the observation counter, the complete running update requires approximately three lines of JavaScript: 

const delta = x - mean;
        mean += delta / count;
        M2 += delta * (x - mean);

The variables count, mean and M2 are initialized to zero before processing the observations.

After at least two observations, the sample variance is obtained as M2 / (count - 1).

Why Use Welford's Algorithm?

Welford's algorithm requires constant additional memory and processes each observation in constant time.

It also avoids repeatedly subtracting two potentially large, nearly equal quantities when computing variance. This makes it generally more numerically stable than the naive one-pass formula based on E[X²] - E[X]².

The recurrence formulas will be used directly in both the interactive demonstration and the sampling experiment in Exercise 2.

Statistical Interpretation

For independent observations from the same population, the sample mean is an unbiased estimator of mu.

The sample variance using the denominator n - 1 is an unbiased estimator of sigma².

Therefore, averaging these estimators across many independent hackers provides empirical values that tend to approach their population counterparts.

The sampling distribution describes how an estimator varies across repeated samples. This is fundamental to inferential statistics, confidence intervals and hypothesis testing.

Unbiasedness describes the expected value across repeated samples. It does not guarantee that a particular finite simulation exactly equals the theoretical parameter. The empirical SD is calculated across the m hacker estimates.
