Exercise 1 — Modes of Convergence

Definitions and Comparison

Let X_n be a sequence of random variables and X a limiting random variable. For the first three modes, the variables are defined on a common probability space.

Four modes of convergence as n → ∞
Mode and definitionMeaning
Convergence in probability

For every ε > 0, P(|X_n − X| > ε) → 0.

 The probability of an error larger than any fixed positive tolerance vanishes.
Almost sure convergence

P(lim_{n→∞} X_n = X) = 1.

 Sample paths converge outside an event of probability zero.
Mean-square convergence (L²)

E[(X_n − X)²] → 0.

 The expected squared error vanishes; in the L² setting the variables have finite second moments.
Convergence in distribution

F_{X_n}(x) → F_X(x) at every continuity point x of F_X.

 Cumulative distribution functions converge at continuity points of the limiting CDF; a common probability space is not required.

Implications, not a linear ranking

  • Almost sure convergence ⇒ convergence in probability.
  • L² convergence ⇒ convergence in probability.
  • Convergence in probability ⇒ convergence in distribution.

The converses do not hold in general. Almost sure convergence and L² convergence are not generally equivalent: neither implies the other without additional assumptions.

3. Law of Large Numbers

For the statements below, let X_1, …, X_n be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with E[X_i] = μ and finite variance σ². Define the sample mean:

X̄_n = (X_1 + … + X_n) / n.

The Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) gives X̄_n → μ in probability.

The Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN) gives X̄_n → μ almost surely under these assumptions. For i.i.d. variables, a finite absolute first moment E[|X_1|] < ∞ already suffices for the SLLN.

These are sufficient assumptions for the versions stated here. Independence is not a universal requirement of all convergence theorems; versions for dependent variables require their own hypotheses.

4. Central Limit Theorem

Under the i.i.d. assumptions above, with 0 < σ² < ∞, the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) states:

√n (X̄_n − μ) / σ → N(0, 1) in distribution.

The sample mean itself converges to μ. The centered mean multiplied by √n instead has a non-degenerate limiting distribution: √n (X̄_n − μ) → N(0, σ²) in distribution. Dividing by σ makes this limit standard normal.

For each independent replica, Z_n = √n (X̄_n − μ) / σ uses the known population μ and σ. The CLT describes distributions of standardized means; it does not make the original observations normal, nor does each standardized realization converge almost surely to a particular normal value. Small n and extreme Bernoulli p may give poor normal approximations.

5. Square-Root Scaling and Variance

Under the stated i.i.d. finite-variance assumptions:

E[X̄_n] = μ.

Var(X̄_n) = σ² / n.

SD(X̄_n) = σ / √n.

For finite-variance variables in general:

Var(X_1 + … + X_n) = ∑_{i=1}^n Var(X_i) + 2 ∑_{i<j} Cov(X_i, X_j).

Variance additivity requires the covariance contribution to vanish; pairwise zero covariances ensure this. Independence guarantees zero covariances when variances exist. Equal variances then give the sample-mean variance above.

The √n factor counteracts the 1/√n decrease in sampling standard deviation, explaining the CLT normalization. These variance identities alone do not prove a CLT.

Variance decreases as 1/n; standard deviation decreases as 1/√n. The table uses the same independent replicas as the LLN experiment. Empirical variances use the sample divisor R − 1, where R is the number of replicas, and empirical SD is their square root.

Independent versus Perfectly Dependent Observations

In a separate comparison, draw one Y per replica from the selected population and set X_1 = … = X_n = Y. Then X̄_n = Y and Var(X̄_n) = σ² for every n, whereas independent observations give σ²/n. Both cases use the same number of replicas; the dependent case uses its own reproducible random stream.

Zero covariance is sufficient for variance additivity; independence is sufficient but not necessary. For perfect dependence, Cov(X_i, X_j) = σ² and Var(Σ X_i) = n²σ². Do not apply the i.i.d. CLT automatically to this dependent case: its mean does not average away randomness when σ² > 0.

Exercise 2 — Conditional Probability & Bayesian Inference

1. Conditional Probability

Conditioning on an event B restricts the reference population to outcomes in B.

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), for P(B) > 0.

P(A | B) and P(B | A) are generally different. Supply coherent event probabilities: max(0, P(A) + P(B) − 1) ≤ P(A ∩ B) ≤ min(P(A), P(B)). A zero conditioning probability makes the corresponding conditional probability undefined.

2. Bayes' Theorem

P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B).

Here P(B) > 0; the displayed event formula also assumes P(A) > 0 so that P(B | A) is defined. Bayes' theorem reverses the direction of conditioning.

For a binary hypothesis H and evidence E:

P(E) = P(E | H) P(H) + P(E | not H) (1 − P(H)).

P(H | E) = P(E | H) P(H) / P(E), when P(E) > 0.

The prior describes probability before the evidence. Each likelihood describes how compatible the evidence is with a hypothesis; it is not the posterior. The posterior updates the hypothesis probability after the evidence. Bayes' theorem does not create information from nothing: the answer depends on the model and probabilities supplied.

Synthetic Diagnostic Test

H means the condition is present; E means the test is positive. The prior is prevalence, P(E | H) is sensitivity and P(E | not H) is the false positive rate. This is a synthetic probability exercise, not an individual diagnostic assessment.

A sensitivity of 95% does not imply a 95% probability of the condition after a positive result. The prevalence and false positive rate also matter.

3. Prior, Likelihood and Posterior

Conditional probabilities above concern events. Bayesian inference uses conditioning to update a distribution over an unknown parameter θ after observing data D.

Prior
The distribution of θ expressing information before observing D.
Likelihood
The probability or density of the observed data, viewed as a function of θ: L(θ; D) = p(D | θ). It is not generally a normalized probability distribution over the parameter.
Posterior
The updated distribution of θ after observing D.

Posterior ∝ Likelihood × Prior.

p(θ | D) = p(D | θ) p(θ) / p(D).

The evidence p(D) normalizes the posterior, provided it is positive and finite. For a continuous parameter, p(D) = ∫ p(D | θ) p(θ) dθ; for a discrete parameter, replace the integral with a sum.

Beta Prior and Bernoulli Data

Let θ ∈ [0,1] be the unknown Bernoulli success probability. Observations are independent conditionally on θ: X_i | θ ~ Bernoulli(θ). The prior is θ ~ Beta(a,b), with a > 0 and b > 0. The shape parameters a and b describe a distribution over θ; they are not θ itself.

L(θ; s,f) ∝ θ^s (1 − θ)^f; n = s + f.

θ | data ~ Beta(a + s, b + f).

E[θ] = a/(a+b); Var(θ) = ab/((a+b)²(a+b+1)).

The prior represents initial uncertainty about θ. The likelihood measures compatibility of the observations with alternative θ values. The posterior combines both: its mean is (a+b)/(a+b+n) times the prior mean plus n/(a+b+n) times s/n when n > 0. For a fixed prior, its influence generally decreases as informative observations accumulate. An unsuitable prior can influence conclusions, especially with few data.

Interactive limits: 0.001 ≤ a,b ≤ 1,000; integer successes and failures from 0 to 10,000 each. These limits bound computational cost and numerical error; the mathematical Beta family permits every positive a,b. Values are displayed to 12 significant digits.

Prior, Normalized Likelihood and Posterior

The original likelihood is not a probability distribution over θ. For shape comparison only, divide θ^s(1−θ)^f by its full-domain integral B(s+1,f+1). The resulting normalized likelihood equals the Beta(s+1,f+1) density; it is not the posterior for a general prior. A binomial coefficient, constant in θ, cancels under this normalization.
