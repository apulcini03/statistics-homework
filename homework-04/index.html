Theoretical Background

Stochastic Penetration Process

Consider n systems and m attackers. Each attacker interacts with a sequence of n systems and receives a score at each step.

For attacker j, let X(j,i) be the score obtained at step i. The cumulative penetration score after k steps is:

S(j,k) = X(j,1) + X(j,2) + ... + X(j,k)

The increments are assumed to be independent and identically distributed within each trajectory and independent across attackers. Each attacker therefore provides one realization of the same stochastic process.

Bernoulli Model

In the Bernoulli model, every increment takes one of two possible values:

X = 1 with probability p, X = 0 with probability 1 - p.

A score of 1 represents a successful penetration attempt, while 0 represents an unsuccessful attempt.

The expected value and variance of a single increment are:

E[X] = p, Var(X) = p(1 - p).

Rademacher Model

In the Rademacher model, every increment takes the value +1 or -1 with equal probability:

P(X = +1) = P(X = -1) = 1/2.

A positive increment represents an increase in the penetration score, while a negative increment represents a decrease.

The model is symmetric, with:

E[X] = 0, Var(X) = 1.

Three Quantities of Interest

We study three quantities at the final step n of each trajectory:

Total score: S(n) = X(1) + ... + X(n).

Average score: A(n) = S(n) / n.

Normalized total score: Z(n) = S(n) / sqrt(n).

These quantities behave differently when the number of systems increases. In particular, their variances scale differently with n.

Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

The Law of Large Numbers states that the average of independent and identically distributed increments converges in probability to their expected value.

S(n) / n converges to E[X] as n increases.

Therefore, in the Bernoulli model, the average score approaches p. In the Rademacher model, it approaches 0.

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The Central Limit Theorem describes the limiting distribution of appropriately standardized sums of independent random variables.

In the Rademacher model, where E[X] = 0 and Var(X) = 1:

S(n) / sqrt(n) converges in distribution to N(0,1).

For the Bernoulli model with 0 < p < 1, standard normal convergence instead requires centering and standardization:

(S(n) - np) / sqrt(np(1-p)) converges in distribution to N(0,1).

Dividing the Bernoulli total score only by sqrt(n) does not produce the same standard normal limit when p is nonzero.

Variance Under Different Normalizations

Let mu = E[X] and sigma² = Var(X). For independent increments:

Var(S(n)) = n sigma².

Var(S(n) / n) = sigma² / n.

Var(S(n) / sqrt(n)) = sigma².

Therefore, the total-score variance increases with n, the average-score variance decreases toward zero, and the normalized-total variance remains constant.

Interpretation and Assumptions

The number of systems n determines the length of each attacker's trajectory. The number of attackers m determines how many independent trajectories are observed.

Increasing n changes the distribution of the cumulative penetration scores. Increasing m gives a larger sample for estimating the distributions.

This is an idealized probabilistic model: real penetration attempts may be dependent, systems may have different vulnerabilities, and attacker behavior may change over time.

Score Distributions

Distribution of the Three Score Quantities

This experiment studies the final scores of all m attackers using the trajectories generated by the stochastic simulator.

The three quantities are S(n), S(n)/n and S(n)/sqrt(n). They are calculated from the same observations, allowing a direct comparison of their distributions.

1. Total Score — S(n)

The total score is the sum of all increments obtained by an attacker across n systems.

In the Bernoulli model, S(n) follows a binomial distribution with parameters n and p. In the Rademacher model, the total is a symmetric random-walk endpoint.

2. Average Score — S(n)/n

Dividing the total score by n produces the average increment per system. As n increases, this quantity concentrates around the expected increment E[X].

This behavior is explained by the Law of Large Numbers.

3. Normalized Total Score — S(n)/sqrt(n)

Dividing the total by sqrt(n) produces a normalization that keeps the variance constant as n increases.

In the Rademacher model, this quantity approaches a standard normal distribution as n becomes large.

In the Bernoulli model with positive p, the normalized total has mean p*sqrt(n), which increases with n. Therefore, it does not converge to a standard normal distribution without centering.

Results and Conclusions

Overview of the Experiments

This homework investigated stochastic penetration processes involving n systems and m independent attackers.

Two models of independent and identically distributed increments were studied: Bernoulli and Rademacher.

For each attacker, three quantities were analyzed: the total score S(n), the average score S(n)/n and the normalized total score S(n)/sqrt(n).

Stochastic Trajectories

In the Bernoulli model, increments are either 0 or 1. Consequently, cumulative scores are non-decreasing and their theoretical expected value grows as n*p.

In the Rademacher model, increments are equally likely to be -1 or +1. Trajectories fluctuate above and below zero, with theoretical expected score equal to zero at every step.

These behaviors were observed in the interactive simulations for both models.

Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers predicts that the average score S(n)/n converges in probability to the expected increment.

In the Bernoulli experiment with p = 0.5, n = 1000 and m = 300, the empirical mean of the averages was approximately 0.5003, compared with the theoretical value 0.5.

In the Rademacher experiment with the same n and m, the empirical mean of the averages was approximately 0.0017, compared with the theoretical value zero.

The observed variances were also close to their theoretical predictions: 0.00025 for Bernoulli and 0.001 for Rademacher at n = 1000.

The empirical proportion of averages differing from their expected value by more than epsilon = 0.1 decreased as the number of systems increased.

These observations illustrate convergence in probability, while Chebyshev's inequality provides a theoretical upper bound on the probability of deviation.

Central Limit Theorem

The Rademacher model has increment mean zero and variance one. Therefore, the Central Limit Theorem predicts convergence in distribution:

S(n)/sqrt(n) converges to N(0,1).

With n = 1000 and m = 1000, the empirical normalized-score mean was approximately 0.0349 and the variance was 1.0042.

Approximately 66.60% of observations fell within [-1, 1], compared with 68.27% under the standard normal model.

Approximately 95.50% fell within [-2, 2], compared with the theoretical reference of 95.45%.

The empirical histogram closely resembled the standard normal density. For smaller n, the normalized score distribution remained more visibly discrete.

Variance and Scaling

The experiments confirmed three different theoretical scaling laws:

Var(S(n)) = n * sigma²

Var(S(n)/n) = sigma² / n

Var(S(n)/sqrt(n)) = sigma²

The total-score variance increased approximately linearly with n, while the average-score variance decreased toward zero.

The normalized-total variance remained approximately constant, around 0.25 for Bernoulli with p = 0.5 and around 1 for Rademacher.

These results explain why different normalizations are useful: division by n reveals concentration around the mean, while division by sqrt(n) preserves a non-vanishing variance.

Effect of Systems and Attackers

The final experiment investigated the aggregate penetration score across all m attackers and n systems.

Let T(n,m) denote the sum of all attacker scores. Under the IID assumptions, its theoretical mean and variance are:

E[T(n,m)] = n*m*mu

Var(T(n,m)) = n*m*sigma²

For Bernoulli with p = 0.5, n = 1000 and m = 1000, the observed aggregate score was 500491, compared with an expected aggregate of 500000.

The resulting pooled average was 0.500491, very close to 0.5.

For Rademacher, the observed aggregate score was 982, compared with an expected value of zero. The pooled average was 0.000982.

Increasing n adds more increments to each attacker's trajectory. Increasing m adds more independent trajectories and observations.

The theoretical variance of the pooled average is sigma²/(n*m), showing that estimation uncertainty decreases as the total number of independent observations increases.

Cybersecurity Interpretation

In the Bernoulli model, the score represents the number of successful penetration attempts. A larger number of systems creates more opportunities for success.

In the Rademacher model, positive and negative increments represent changes in an abstract penetration score, rather than literal counts of compromised systems.

The number of attackers determines how many independent penetration trajectories are simulated. More attackers improve the precision of empirical distribution estimates.

The model assumes identical success probabilities and independent events. Real cybersecurity environments may involve different vulnerability levels, correlated failures, shared attack techniques and adaptive attackers.

Limitations of the Simulations

All results are based on finite Monte Carlo simulations. Empirical means, variances and probabilities fluctuate between independent executions.

The systems-and-attackers experiment uses a fixed pseudo-random seed and nested samples for reproducibility. Its observed numerical values describe that specific experiment.

The LLN and CLT are mathematical results established under specified assumptions; simulations illustrate their predictions but cannot independently prove the theorems.

Final Conclusion

This homework demonstrated how normalization affects the statistical behavior of stochastic penetration scores.

The average score converged toward its theoretical expectation, illustrating the Law of Large Numbers.

The normalized Rademacher total approached the standard normal distribution, illustrating the Central Limit Theorem.

The experiments also confirmed the different variance scaling laws and showed how the number of systems and attackers influences aggregate scores and estimation uncertainty.

The main lesson is that large-scale stochastic behavior can be studied through probability distributions, convergence and normalization, provided the assumptions of the underlying model are understood.
