Private Key to Public Key in Ethereum

Demonstrate how elliptic-curve scalar multiplication transforms a private key k into a public key Q = kG.

The experiment uses the toy curve y² = x³ + 7 (mod 17), with base point G = (1, 5). The goal is to understand the algorithm, not to implement a secure cryptographic system.

The application includes point addition, scalar multiplication, step-by-step visualization, and statistical exploration of coordinate frequencies, uniformity, bit distributions, correlations, and avalanche-like behaviour.

Theoretical Background

Elliptic Curve Cryptography

Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) is a form of public-key cryptography based on mathematical operations involving points on elliptic curves over finite fields.

A private key is an integer k, while the public key is a point Q obtained through elliptic-curve scalar multiplication:

Q = kG

Here, G is a publicly known base point. Scalar multiplication consists of repeatedly adding G to itself according to the elliptic curve point-addition rules.

The Toy Elliptic Curve

This application uses the curve:

y² = x³ + 7 (mod 17)

The base point is G = (1, 5). Since computations are performed modulo 17, the coordinates belong to the finite field F17.

The point G has order 9, meaning that 9G = O, where O is the point at infinity. Therefore, the generated points repeat every nine scalar multiplications.

The One-Way Property

Computing Q = kG is efficient, while recovering k from Q is computationally difficult for appropriately chosen cryptographic curves and sufficiently large parameters. This is known as the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP).

Our toy curve is not cryptographically secure: its small subgroup makes it easy to recover the private key by testing possible values.

Connection to Ethereum

Ethereum uses the secp256k1 elliptic curve for its traditional externally owned account signature scheme. Its equation has the same general form: y² = x³ + 7, but calculations are performed over a much larger finite field.

This application demonstrates the underlying scalar multiplication principle, without implementing real Ethereum cryptography, addresses or digital signatures.

Results and Conclusions

Coordinate Distributions

For private keys k = 1, ..., 8, the X coordinates take only four values: 1, 2, 5 and 12. Each occurs twice, while the eight Y coordinates are all distinct.

Therefore, the coordinates are not uniformly distributed over the entire field F17. Their distributions are constrained by the algebraic structure of the elliptic curve.

Uniformity and Periodicity

The base point G has order 9, so the generated public points repeat every nine scalar multiplications: (k + 9)G = kG.

Uniformly selecting k from 1 to 9 produces a uniform distribution over the nine elements of the subgroup generated by G, including the point at infinity. Uniformity of points does not imply uniformity of their coordinates over the full finite field.

Bit Distribution

Using a fixed 5-bit representation for each coordinate, the experiment with k = 1, ..., 8 produces 12 one-bits out of 40 for X (30%), and 14 one-bits out of 40 for Y (35%).

This imbalance depends on the small curve, the generated subgroup and the chosen binary encoding. Bit frequencies alone do not establish cryptographic security.

Input-Output Correlation

Pearson correlation measures linear relationships between the private key k and the public key coordinates.

A small correlation coefficient does not imply that the output is independent of the input. Scalar multiplication is deterministic, and its algebraic relationships cannot be fully described by linear correlation.

Avalanche-like Behaviour

Flipping a single bit of the private key can change multiple bits in the encoded public point. For example, changing k = 6 into k = 7 changes 7 out of 10 output bits (70%).

This observation is specific to the selected keys and the fixed 10-bit point encoding. It does not demonstrate the strict avalanche criterion associated with cryptographic hash functions.

Scalar Multiplication vs Hash Functions

Elliptic-curve scalar multiplication and cryptographic hash functions are different mathematical constructions.

On appropriately selected large curves, scalar multiplication is efficiently computable in the forward direction, while recovering the private scalar is believed to be computationally infeasible because of the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP).

Cryptographic hash functions are instead designed to provide properties such as preimage resistance, second-preimage resistance and collision resistance. Avalanche behaviour is also a commonly studied characteristic of hash functions.

Scalar multiplication is not a hash function and should not be evaluated using hash-function criteria alone.

Final Conclusion

This experiment demonstrates how an elliptic curve private key generates a public point through scalar multiplication.

The statistical results highlight periodicity, coordinate constraints and encoding-dependent bit patterns. The small toy curve is insecure by design, but it provides a useful illustration of the mathematical principles behind elliptic-curve public-key cryptography.
