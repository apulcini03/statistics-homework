Theoretical Background

Joint, Marginal and Conditional Probabilities

Let X and Y be discrete random variables. The joint probability describes a pair of values; marginal probabilities describe each variable separately.

P(X = x, Y = y) is the joint probability.

P(X = x) = ∑y P(X = x, Y = y).

P(Y = y) = ∑x P(X = x, Y = y).

P(Y = y | X = x) = P(X = x, Y = y) / P(X = x), provided P(X = x) > 0.

Independence in Probability

X and Y are independent if their joint probabilities factor into their marginal probabilities for every pair of possible values:

P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) P(Y = y) for all x and y.

Equivalently, whenever P(X = x) > 0, independence means P(Y = y | X = x) = P(Y = y) for every y.

Population Probabilities and Sample Frequencies

Statistical independence is a hypothesis about the joint distribution in the underlying population. Sample frequencies are observed counts, not population probabilities. With n paired observations, a contingency table records counts nij for combinations of the categories xi and yj; its margins give the row and column totals.

The relative frequency nij / n estimates P(X = xi, Y = yj). Sampling variation can produce differences from independence even when the population variables are independent. Inference requires an appropriate sampling design; a usual contingency-table test assumes independent observations and sufficiently large expected cell counts for its chi-square approximation.

Hypotheses and Interpretation

H₀: X and Y are independent in the population; the factorization above holds for every category pair.

H₁: X and Y are associated in the population; the factorization fails for at least one category pair.

Failure to reject H₀ does not prove independence: the sample may provide insufficient evidence to detect an association. Statistical association does not establish causation; confounding or selection effects may explain an observed relationship.
